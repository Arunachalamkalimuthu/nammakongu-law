Mutual consent divorce is the quickest and least contested way to end a marriage. Both spouses file together, and the court's role is to confirm that consent is genuine and that every issue between them is settled.

## Who can file

For Hindus, Buddhists, Jains and Sikhs, Section 13B of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 requires that:

- the spouses have **lived separately for one year or more** before filing,
- they **have not been able to live together**, and
- they have **mutually agreed** that the marriage should be dissolved.

"Living separately" means not living as husband and wife. It does not require separate houses.

Other laws provide similar remedies. Marriages registered under the Special Marriage Act, 1954 use Section 28, which also requires one year of separation. Christian couples use Section 10A of the Divorce Act, 1869, which requires two years of separation.

## The two motions

1. **First motion.** Both spouses file a joint petition with supporting affidavits and appear before the family court. The court records their statements.
2. **Waiting period.** The law sets a gap of at least six months to allow reconsideration. The petition lapses if the second motion is not moved within eighteen months of filing.
3. **Second motion.** Both spouses appear again and confirm consent. If the court is satisfied, it passes the decree of divorce.

## Waiving the six-month wait

In **Amardeep Singh v. Harveen Kaur (2017)** the Supreme Court held that the six-month period is directory, not mandatory. The family court can waive it where:

- the one year of separation and the six-month period together have already passed before the first motion,
- mediation and conciliation have failed,
- the spouses have settled alimony, custody and all other pending issues, and
- waiting would only prolong their hardship.

An application for waiver can be filed once the first motion is made. In **Shilpa Sailesh v. Varun Sreenivasan (2023)** the Supreme Court also held that it can itself dissolve a marriage on the ground of irretrievable breakdown under Article 142. That power belongs to the Supreme Court only, not to family courts.

## What the settlement should cover

The court expects the terms to be complete before the decree. A typical settlement covers:

- permanent alimony, as a lump sum or instalments
- return of jewellery, stridhan and other belongings
- custody of and visitation with children, and their maintenance
- withdrawal of other pending cases between the spouses, such as maintenance or domestic violence petitions

## Documents

- Marriage certificate or invitation, and wedding photographs
- Address and identity proof of both spouses
- Proof of separate residence for the past year
- Income details and the signed settlement terms
- Passport-size photographs

## Which court

Section 19 of the Hindu Marriage Act lets the petition be filed where the marriage took place, where the couple last lived together, or where either spouse lives. In districts with a family court, the petition goes there.
